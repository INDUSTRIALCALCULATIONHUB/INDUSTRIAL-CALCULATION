What Is Pump Curves and System Curves?

Pump curves show how a specific pump responds to changes in flow, while system curves show the head required by the connected system. Their intersection identifies the expected operating point.

Why Is It Important in Engineering?

A pump normally produces different head, efficiency, power and NPSH behaviour at different flows. A system requires static head plus flow-dependent losses; changing resistance or speed moves the operating point.

Use the stated basis.Identify the actual fluid, material, geometry, operating condition and relevant equipment boundary before using any engineering relation or reference value.

Key Terms and Definitions

Pump curve
Vendor-tested relationship between flow and head for a stated pump and speed.
System curve
Head requirement as a function of flow for the connected system.
Best efficiency point
Operating region of highest tested efficiency.
Control curve
Operating relationship after valve, speed or bypass control changes.

Fundamental Principle

A pump normally produces different head, efficiency, power and NPSH behaviour at different flows. A system requires static head plus flow-dependent losses; changing resistance or speed moves the operating point.

Formulae, Symbols and Units

System head

Hsystem = Hstatic + KQ²

A simplified expression for systems dominated by turbulent-flow losses.

Affinity-law screening

Q ∝ N; H ∝ N²; P ∝ N³

Use only for geometrically similar operation within the pump maker’s stated range.

Unit consistency

Use a single, declared unit system. Confirm that all dimensions, properties and temperatures refer to the same service condition before combining them in a calculation.

Assumptions and Validity Range

  • Curves come from the correct pump, impeller diameter and rotational speed.
  • The system curve contains the complete operating route.
  • Parallel and series pumps are analysed with their correct combined curves.

Factors Affecting the Result

Primary factor

Impeller diameter, speed and liquid density.

Service factor

Static head and system resistance.

System factor

Valve position, parallel paths and pump arrangement.

Step-by-Step Engineering Method

  1. Define all required operating cases.
  2. Build system curves from static head and loss calculations.
  3. Obtain vendor pump curves at the correct speed and impeller diameter.
  4. Locate candidate operating points and check efficiency, power and NPSH.
  5. Evaluate control, turndown, minimum flow and future cases.

Illustrative Engineering Example

Hypothetical example — not a design calculation

A control valve that throttles a pump changes the system resistance and moves the duty to lower flow. Compare this with speed control only using correct pump and system curves.

Use project data, applicable standards, supplier information and qualified review before making a design, procurement, construction or operating decision.

Industrial Applications

  • Pump selection and motor sizing.
  • Variable-speed pump studies.
  • Parallel and standby-pump evaluation.
  • Commissioning and troubleshooting.

Common Mistakes and Limitations

Do not extend a preliminary relation beyond its basis.Published equations and screening methods need appropriate geometry, operating data, source limits and independent engineering review for actual project use.
  • Treating the design flow as guaranteed without a system curve.
  • Using affinity laws outside their valid range.
  • Ignoring minimum flow or motor overload.
  • Comparing curves at different impeller diameters or speeds.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the operating point?

It is the intersection of the applicable pump and system curves.

Can a valve increase pump head?

Throttling changes resistance and may move the point to higher pump head but lower flow.

Are pump curves universal?

No. They are specific to a tested pump configuration, speed and liquid basis.
