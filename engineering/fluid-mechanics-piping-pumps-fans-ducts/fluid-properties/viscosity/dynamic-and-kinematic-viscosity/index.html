What Is Dynamic and Kinematic Viscosity?

Dynamic viscosity describes a fluid’s resistance to shear, while kinematic viscosity relates that resistance to density. Both properties affect pressure loss, flow regime, pumping and heat-transfer calculations.

Why Is It Important in Engineering?

A fluid shears when adjacent layers move at different velocities. Dynamic viscosity links shear stress to velocity gradient; kinematic viscosity is dynamic viscosity divided by density and is often used in fluid-flow correlations.

Use the stated basis.Identify the actual fluid, material, geometry, operating condition and relevant equipment boundary before using any engineering relation or reference value.

Key Terms and Definitions

Dynamic viscosity, μ
Resistance to shear; SI unit Pa·s.
Kinematic viscosity, ν
Dynamic viscosity divided by density; SI unit m²/s.
Newtonian fluid
Fluid with viscosity that is substantially independent of shear rate.
Temperature basis
The stated temperature at which property data apply.

Fundamental Principle

A fluid shears when adjacent layers move at different velocities. Dynamic viscosity links shear stress to velocity gradient; kinematic viscosity is dynamic viscosity divided by density and is often used in fluid-flow correlations.

Formulae, Symbols and Units

Newton’s law of viscosity

τ = μ (du/dy)

Shear stress τ is proportional to velocity gradient du/dy for a Newtonian fluid.

Kinematic viscosity

ν = μ / ρ

Use dynamic viscosity μ and density ρ at the same temperature and pressure.

Unit consistency

Use a single, declared unit system. Confirm that all dimensions, properties and temperatures refer to the same service condition before combining them in a calculation.

Assumptions and Validity Range

  • Properties represent the actual fluid composition and temperature.
  • The Newtonian approximation is suitable where it is used.
  • The selected viscosity form matches the engineering method.

Factors Affecting the Result

Primary factor

Temperature, which can substantially alter liquid viscosity.

Service factor

Composition, concentration and contamination.

System factor

Shear rate for non-Newtonian materials such as slurries or polymer solutions.

Step-by-Step Engineering Method

  1. Define fluid composition, temperature and pressure.
  2. Obtain a traceable dynamic or kinematic viscosity value at that condition.
  3. Select the characteristic velocity and geometry for the planned calculation.
  4. Use the property consistently in Reynolds-number, loss or heat-transfer methods.
  5. Check whether non-Newtonian behaviour or temperature variation requires a specialised method.

Illustrative Engineering Example

Hypothetical example — not a design calculation

For a liquid line, obtain viscosity at the operating temperature before calculating Reynolds number and friction factor. A room-temperature property should not be substituted for a heated or cooled process stream.

Use project data, applicable standards, supplier information and qualified review before making a design, procurement, construction or operating decision.

Industrial Applications

  • Pipe and duct pressure-loss assessment.
  • Pump and line sizing screening.
  • Reynolds-number and friction-factor selection.
  • Heat-exchanger and mixing calculations.

Common Mistakes and Limitations

Do not extend a preliminary relation beyond its basis.Published equations and screening methods need appropriate geometry, operating data, source limits and independent engineering review for actual project use.
  • Using a viscosity value at the wrong temperature.
  • Confusing dynamic and kinematic viscosity.
  • Assuming all fluids are Newtonian.
  • Mixing units such as cP, Pa·s and m²/s without conversion.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do gases have viscosity?

Yes. Gas viscosity also affects flow and heat-transfer behaviour, although its temperature trend differs from many liquids.

Which viscosity should be used for Reynolds number?

Use either dynamic viscosity with density or kinematic viscosity directly, but keep all data at the same condition.

Why is viscosity important for pumping?

It affects friction loss, pump performance and the suitability of correlations used for the system.
