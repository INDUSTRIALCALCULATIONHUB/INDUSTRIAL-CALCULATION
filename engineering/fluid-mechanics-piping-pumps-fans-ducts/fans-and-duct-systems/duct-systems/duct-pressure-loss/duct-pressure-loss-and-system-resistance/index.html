What Is Duct Pressure Loss and System Resistance?

Duct-system resistance is the pressure loss created by straight duct, fittings, filters, terminals, dampers and equipment. It determines the pressure a fan must develop at the required airflow.

Why Is It Important in Engineering?

Airflow through ducts causes distributed wall friction and local disturbance losses. Since many losses rise approximately with the square of flow in turbulent service, small airflow changes can significantly shift fan duty.

Use the stated basis.Identify the actual fluid, material, geometry, operating condition and relevant equipment boundary before using any engineering relation or reference value.

Key Terms and Definitions

Hydraulic diameter
Four times flow area divided by wetted perimeter for a non-circular duct.
Velocity pressure
Dynamic pressure based on density and mean velocity.
Static pressure loss
Pressure decrease associated with friction and local resistance.
System resistance
Total pressure requirement of the connected duct path.

Fundamental Principle

Airflow through ducts causes distributed wall friction and local disturbance losses. Since many losses rise approximately with the square of flow in turbulent service, small airflow changes can significantly shift fan duty.

Formulae, Symbols and Units

Duct loss relation

Δp = f (L/Dh) (ρv²/2) + ΣK(ρv²/2)

Use hydraulic diameter Dh and the correct local-loss velocity basis.

System relation

Δp ≈ KQ²

A screening form for a fixed duct configuration in turbulent-flow conditions.

Unit consistency

Use a single, declared unit system. Confirm that all dimensions, properties and temperatures refer to the same service condition before combining them in a calculation.

Assumptions and Validity Range

  • Duct dimensions represent internal flow area.
  • Loss coefficients match the actual fitting geometry.
  • Air density is appropriate to the operating temperature and pressure.

Factors Affecting the Result

Primary factor

Duct size, shape, roughness and straight length.

Service factor

Elbows, branches, transitions and dampers.

System factor

Air density, flow, filter loading and terminal conditions.

Step-by-Step Engineering Method

  1. Define airflow cases, air condition and full duct route.
  2. Calculate velocities in each section from its actual area.
  3. Estimate straight-duct friction and each local loss.
  4. Add filters, equipment, outlets and control-device losses.
  5. Develop a system curve and compare it with the selected fan curve.

Illustrative Engineering Example

Hypothetical example — not a design calculation

A clean filter and a loaded filter represent different duct-system curves. Evaluate both so the fan, motor and control arrangement accommodate expected loading.

Industrial Applications

  • Dust-collection and fume-extraction systems.
  • HVAC and process ventilation.
  • Combustion-air and exhaust ducts.
  • Fan and damper selection.

Common Mistakes and Limitations

Do not extend a preliminary relation beyond its basis.Published equations and screening methods need appropriate geometry, operating data, source limits and independent engineering review for actual project use.
  • Using one velocity for all branches.
  • Ignoring filter loading and equipment pressure drop.
  • Applying round-duct coefficients to an unrelated rectangular fitting.
  • Treating a duct-pressure calculation as a final noise or structural design.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does a filter affect fan duty?

Its pressure loss contributes to total system resistance and often rises as it loads.

Does larger duct always reduce system cost?

It reduces velocity loss but may increase material, space and support cost.

What is hydraulic diameter for?

It provides a characteristic dimension for non-circular duct flow calculations.
