Pressure Vessel Design Basics

Learn the fundamentals of pressure vessel design, including hoop stress, longitudinal stress, thin and thick-walled vessels, ASME design principles, material selection, safety factors and practical engineering applications.

Last Updated: July 26, 2026

What is a Pressure Vessel?

A pressure vessel is a closed container specifically designed to safely store or process liquids and gases at pressures significantly higher or lower than atmospheric pressure. Pressure vessels are essential components in power plants, refineries, petrochemical facilities, fertilizer plants, pharmaceutical industries, food processing units and compressed air systems.

Unlike ordinary storage tanks, pressure vessels are subjected to substantial internal or external pressures that generate stresses within the vessel wall. Engineers must therefore carefully design the vessel geometry, wall thickness, material, weld joints and support arrangements to ensure structural integrity throughout its service life.

Modern pressure vessels are designed in accordance with internationally recognized standards such as the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC), ensuring safe operation under specified pressure, temperature and loading conditions.

Primary Stresses in Pressure Vessels

Internal pressure generates different types of stresses within the vessel wall. Understanding these stresses is fundamental to safe pressure vessel design.

Stress Type Description Importance
Hoop Stress Circumferential stress acting around the vessel. Highest stress in thin cylinders.
Longitudinal Stress Stress acting parallel to the vessel axis. Approximately half the hoop stress.
Radial Stress Stress acting through the wall thickness. Usually negligible for thin vessels.
Thermal Stress Stress caused by temperature gradients. Important in high-temperature equipment.

Basic Pressure Vessel Design Equations

For thin-walled cylindrical pressure vessels, where the wall thickness is less than approximately one-tenth of the internal diameter, the following equations are commonly used.

  • Hoop Stress: σh = (P × D) / (2t)
  • Longitudinal Stress: σl = (P × D) / (4t)

Where:

  • P = Internal pressure (Pa or MPa)
  • D = Internal diameter (m or mm)
  • t = Wall thickness (m or mm)
  • σ = Stress (MPa)

These equations are widely used for preliminary design and educational purposes. Final engineering design should always follow applicable design codes such as the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code.

Worked Engineering Example

Consider a thin cylindrical pressure vessel with an internal pressure of 2 MPa, an internal diameter of 1000 mm and a wall thickness of 12 mm.

Hoop Stress

σ = (2 × 1000) / (2 × 12)

σ = 83.3 MPa

This example illustrates why hoop stress governs the design of thin-walled pressure vessels. Engineers compare the calculated stress with the allowable stress specified by the design code and selected material.

Thin-Walled vs Thick-Walled Pressure Vessels

Feature Thin-Walled Vessel Thick-Walled Vessel
Wall Thickness Less than about D/20 Greater than about D/20
Stress Distribution Nearly uniform Varies across wall thickness
Analysis Method Thin cylinder equations Lame's equations
Typical Applications Storage vessels, air receivers High-pressure reactors, hydraulic cylinders

Pressure Vessel Safety Considerations

Pressure vessels must be designed with adequate safety margins because failure can have catastrophic consequences. Design engineers consider numerous factors beyond internal pressure to ensure safe operation.

  • Allowable stress limits
  • Corrosion allowance
  • Joint efficiency of welded seams
  • Fatigue loading
  • Thermal expansion
  • Pressure surges
  • Wind and seismic loads
  • Hydrostatic testing requirements

Engineering Design Practices

Professional pressure vessel design involves much more than calculating stresses. Engineers must evaluate operating pressure, design temperature, corrosion rate, fabrication method, inspection requirements and applicable design standards before determining the final vessel dimensions.

International standards such as the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code provide detailed rules for material selection, allowable stresses, joint efficiency, testing, inspection and certification to ensure safe and reliable operation throughout the equipment's service life.

Conclusion

Pressure vessel design is a critical discipline in mechanical and process engineering, ensuring the safe containment of pressurized fluids under varying operating conditions. Understanding hoop stress, longitudinal stress, material selection, wall thickness and applicable design codes forms the foundation of reliable pressure vessel engineering.

Although simplified stress equations are useful for preliminary analysis and educational purposes, the final design of pressure vessels should always comply with recognized standards such as the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code. Proper engineering design, fabrication, inspection and maintenance help maximize equipment reliability, operational safety and service life.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

What is a pressure vessel?

A pressure vessel is a closed container designed to safely hold gases or liquids at pressures different from atmospheric pressure.

Which stress is highest in a thin cylindrical pressure vessel?

Hoop stress is the highest principal stress and is typically twice the longitudinal stress.

What code is commonly used for pressure vessel design?

The ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC) is one of the most widely accepted international standards for pressure vessel design.

Why is corrosion allowance provided?

Corrosion allowance compensates for expected material loss during service, ensuring the vessel maintains adequate strength throughout its design life.

What is the difference between thin and thick pressure vessels?

Thin-walled vessels have relatively small wall thickness compared with their diameter and use simplified stress equations, whereas thick-walled vessels require more advanced stress analysis because stress varies significantly through the wall thickness.
