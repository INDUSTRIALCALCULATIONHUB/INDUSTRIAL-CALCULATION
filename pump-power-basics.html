Pump Power Basics: Hydraulic Power, Pump Efficiency & Motor Selection

Learn the fundamentals of pump power, hydraulic power calculations, pump efficiency, brake power, total dynamic head (TDH), motor sizing and practical engineering applications in water supply, HVAC, industrial process, power and utility systems.

Last Updated: July 26, 2026

What is Pump Power?

Pump power is the amount of energy required to move a liquid through a piping system while overcoming elevation differences, pipe friction, fittings, valves and pressure requirements. It is one of the most important design parameters in hydraulic engineering because it determines the size of the pump, motor and electrical supply required for reliable operation.

Engineers use pump power calculations when designing water distribution systems, cooling water circuits, boiler feed systems, chemical process plants, irrigation networks, wastewater treatment facilities and fire protection systems. Proper pump selection improves energy efficiency, reduces operating costs and extends equipment life.

Hydraulic Power Equation

The hydraulic power delivered to a fluid is calculated using:

  • P = ρ × g × Q × H
  • P = Hydraulic power (W)
  • ρ = Fluid density (kg/m³)
  • g = Gravitational acceleration (9.81 m/s²)
  • Q = Flow rate (m³/s)
  • H = Total Dynamic Head (m)

Because pumps are not 100% efficient, the shaft power required is:

  • Brake Power = Hydraulic Power / Pump Efficiency

Worked Engineering Example

A centrifugal pump delivers water at a flow rate of 0.05 m³/s against a total dynamic head of 40 m. Pump efficiency is 80%.

Hydraulic Power = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.05 × 40

Hydraulic Power = 19.62 kW

Brake Power = 19.62 / 0.80

Brake Power = 24.5 kW

A standard 30 kW motor would normally be selected to provide an adequate safety margin.

Total Dynamic Head (TDH)

Total Dynamic Head is the total head the pump must overcome and is one of the most important parameters in pump selection.

  • Static suction head
  • Static discharge head
  • Pipe friction losses
  • Valve and fitting losses
  • Required discharge pressure

Underestimating TDH results in insufficient flow, while overestimating it can lead to oversized pumps and unnecessary energy consumption.

Pump Efficiency

Pump efficiency represents how effectively a pump converts mechanical input power into useful hydraulic power.

Pump Type Typical Efficiency
Small Centrifugal Pump 50–70%
Large Centrifugal Pump 75–90%
Multistage Pump 70–88%
Positive Displacement Pump 80–95%

Factors Affecting Pump Power

  • Flow Rate: Higher flow requires more power.
  • Total Dynamic Head: Increasing head increases power demand.
  • Fluid Density: Denser fluids require greater hydraulic power.
  • Pump Efficiency: Higher efficiency reduces motor power requirements.
  • Pipe Friction: Greater friction losses increase pump head.
  • Fluid Viscosity: Viscous fluids require additional pumping energy.
  • System Layout: Long pipelines and numerous fittings increase pressure losses.

Motor Selection Considerations

Motor selection should always consider brake power, starting current, service factor, ambient temperature, duty cycle and future operating conditions. Engineers typically select a motor with adequate reserve capacity rather than operating continuously at full load.

Engineering Best Practices

  • Select pumps near the Best Efficiency Point (BEP).
  • Minimize pipe friction losses.
  • Avoid excessive pump oversizing.
  • Maintain proper suction conditions.
  • Prevent cavitation.
  • Use variable frequency drives where appropriate.
  • Inspect impellers regularly.
  • Monitor vibration and bearing temperature.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

What is hydraulic power?

Hydraulic power is the useful energy transferred from the pump to the fluid.

Why is pump efficiency important?

Higher efficiency reduces electrical power consumption and operating costs.

What is Total Dynamic Head?

Total Dynamic Head is the total resistance the pump must overcome, including elevation, pressure and friction losses.

Why should pumps operate near the Best Efficiency Point?

Operating near the BEP minimizes vibration, improves reliability and reduces energy consumption.

Conclusion

Pump power calculations form the foundation of efficient hydraulic system design. Understanding hydraulic power, brake power, total dynamic head, pump efficiency and motor selection enables engineers to design reliable, energy-efficient pumping systems for industrial and utility applications.

By applying sound engineering principles and selecting pumps that operate near their best efficiency point, organizations can reduce energy consumption, improve equipment reliability and lower lifecycle operating costs.
