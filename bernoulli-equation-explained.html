Bernoulli Equation: Formula, Assumptions & Engineering Applications

Understand Bernoulli's Equation, its derivation, assumptions, practical energy equation, engineering applications, limitations and real-world examples used in fluid mechanics and industrial piping systems.

Last Updated: July 31, 2026

Introduction

Bernoulli’s Equation is one of the most fundamental principles in fluid mechanics used to understand the relationship between pressure, velocity, and elevation in a flowing fluid. It is widely applied in engineering systems involving liquid and gas flow, including pipelines, pumps, turbines, nozzles, compressors, and various industrial fluid handling equipment.

In practical engineering applications, understanding fluid energy changes is essential for designing efficient flow systems. Bernoulli’s Equation helps engineers analyze how pressure changes when fluid velocity or elevation varies along a flow path. For example, an increase in fluid velocity generally results in a decrease in static pressure, provided other conditions remain constant.

The equation is based on the conservation of energy principle and represents the conversion of energy between pressure energy, kinetic energy, and potential energy within a flowing fluid. It provides a simplified approach for analyzing fluid flow behavior and serves as a foundation for more advanced calculations involving pumps, pressure losses, and hydraulic systems.

This article explains Bernoulli’s Equation, its terms, assumptions, derivation, engineering applications, limitations, and practical importance in industrial fluid flow systems. It is intended as a reference for engineers, designers, students, and professionals involved in mechanical, chemical, civil, and process engineering.

What is Bernoulli's Equation?

Bernoulli's Equation is a mathematical expression based on the principle of conservation of energy in fluid flow. It describes the relationship between pressure energy, kinetic energy, and potential energy of a fluid moving along a streamline. The equation states that the total mechanical energy of an ideal flowing fluid remains constant when there is no energy loss due to friction or other disturbances.

In simple terms, Bernoulli's Equation explains how changes in fluid velocity, pressure, and elevation are interconnected. When a fluid flows through a restricted section of a pipe, its velocity increases and the pressure decreases. Similarly, when fluid moves to a higher elevation, some of its energy is converted into potential energy, affecting the pressure and velocity.

The general form of Bernoulli's Equation is derived from the conservation of energy principle and is widely used in fluid mechanics for analyzing flow behavior in pipes, ducts, nozzles, pumps, turbines, and other fluid handling systems.

Bernoulli's Equation is applicable for steady, incompressible, and frictionless fluid flow conditions. Although real industrial systems involve friction and energy losses, the equation provides a fundamental understanding of fluid behavior and forms the basis for more advanced flow calculations.

History and Bernoulli's Principle

Bernoulli's Principle was introduced by Swiss mathematician and physicist Daniel Bernoulli in 1738 through his work "Hydrodynamica". His research established the relationship between fluid pressure and velocity, which became one of the fundamental concepts in fluid mechanics.

The principle is based on the conservation of energy concept and states that for an ideal fluid flowing steadily along a streamline, the total energy per unit weight remains constant. This energy consists of pressure energy, kinetic energy, and potential energy.

Daniel Bernoulli's work provided the foundation for understanding fluid movement and pressure variations in flowing systems. Later developments by engineers and scientists extended the principle to practical applications by including factors such as friction losses, pumps, turbines, and other energy interactions.

Today, Bernoulli's Principle is widely used in mechanical, civil, chemical, and aerospace engineering. It helps engineers analyze fluid behavior in pipelines, aircraft wings, hydraulic systems, ventilation systems, and various industrial flow applications.

Bernoulli Equation (Ideal Form)

The ideal form of Bernoulli’s Equation represents the conservation of mechanical energy for a fluid flowing steadily without any energy losses. It assumes that the fluid is incompressible, the flow is steady, and there is no friction or heat transfer affecting the system.

The ideal Bernoulli Equation is expressed as:

P₁/ρg + V₁²/2g + Z₁ = P₂/ρg + V₂²/2g + Z₂

Where the three terms represent the different forms of mechanical energy available in a flowing fluid:

  • Pressure Head (P/ρg): Energy due to fluid pressure.
  • Velocity Head (V²/2g): Energy due to fluid motion or velocity.
  • Elevation Head (Z): Potential energy due to the height of fluid above a reference level.

According to the ideal Bernoulli Equation, the total head of a fluid remains constant along a streamline. If one form of energy increases, another form must decrease to maintain the total energy balance.

For example, when fluid flows through a pipe with a reduced cross-sectional area, the velocity increases due to continuity principles. The increase in velocity head is balanced by a reduction in pressure head, resulting in lower static pressure at that location.

Although the ideal equation does not consider practical losses, it provides the basic understanding required for analyzing real fluid flow systems and serves as the foundation for modified Bernoulli equations used in engineering design.

Meaning of Each Term

Bernoulli’s Equation consists of three main energy components: pressure energy, velocity energy, and elevation energy. Understanding the meaning of each term is essential for analyzing fluid flow behavior in engineering systems.

The ideal Bernoulli Equation is written as:

P/ρg + V²/2g + Z = Constant

Each term represents energy per unit weight of fluid and is expressed as a head in metres of fluid column.

  • Pressure Head (P/ρg): Pressure head represents the energy stored in a fluid due to its pressure. It indicates the height of a fluid column that can be supported by the available pressure.
  • Velocity Head (V²/2g): Velocity head represents the kinetic energy of a moving fluid. It depends on the fluid velocity and increases as the flow velocity increases.
  • Elevation Head (Z): Elevation head represents the potential energy of a fluid due to its position above a reference elevation. A higher fluid elevation results in higher potential energy.

The sum of these three energy components represents the total head of the fluid. In an ideal flow system, the total head remains constant along a streamline, meaning energy is continuously converted between pressure, velocity, and elevation forms.

In practical engineering systems, additional factors such as friction losses, fittings, valves, pumps, and turbines affect the energy balance. Therefore, real-world applications use a modified form of Bernoulli’s Equation that includes head losses and energy additions or removals.

Assumptions

Bernoulli’s Equation is based on certain assumptions that simplify fluid flow analysis. These assumptions define the conditions under which the ideal form of the equation can be applied accurately.

  • Steady Flow: The fluid properties such as pressure, velocity, and density at any point do not change with time. The flow conditions remain constant during the analysis.
  • Incompressible Fluid: The fluid density remains constant throughout the flow process. This assumption is generally valid for liquids and low-speed gas flows.
  • Frictionless Flow: Energy losses due to friction between the fluid and pipe walls or other components are neglected.
  • Flow Along a Streamline: The equation is applied between two points located on the same streamline where the fluid particles follow a continuous flow path.
  • No Energy Addition or Removal: The basic equation assumes that there are no pumps, turbines, or other devices adding or removing energy from the fluid between the two points.
  • Negligible Heat Transfer: Heat transfer effects are assumed to be insignificant, and the fluid energy changes occur only due to pressure, velocity, and elevation variations.

These assumptions make Bernoulli’s Equation easier to apply for theoretical analysis. However, actual industrial systems usually involve friction losses, flow disturbances, and energy transfer equipment. For such systems, the practical Bernoulli Equation with head loss terms is used.

Practical Bernoulli Equation (with Head Loss)

In real engineering systems, fluid flow is affected by friction, pipe roughness, fittings, valves, bends, and other components that cause energy losses. Therefore, the ideal Bernoulli Equation is modified by including head loss and energy transfer terms to represent actual operating conditions.

The practical form of Bernoulli’s Equation is:

P₁/ρg + V₁²/2g + Z₁ + hₚ = P₂/ρg + V₂²/2g + Z₂ + hₗ + hₜ

Where:

  • P₁ and P₂: Pressure at points 1 and 2 respectively (Pa).
  • ρ: Fluid density (kg/m³).
  • g: Acceleration due to gravity (9.81 m/s²).
  • V₁ and V₂: Fluid velocity at points 1 and 2 (m/s).
  • Z₁ and Z₂: Elevation of points 1 and 2 above a reference level (m).
  • hₚ: Head added by pumps or other energy input devices (m).
  • hₗ: Head loss due to friction and minor losses in the system (m).
  • hₜ: Head removed by turbines or energy extraction devices (m).

The head loss term accounts for the reduction in available energy caused by friction inside pipes and losses due to fittings such as elbows, valves, reducers, and expansions. In industrial systems, these losses are important factors in pump selection, pipeline design, and energy consumption calculations.

The practical Bernoulli Equation is widely used in engineering applications such as water distribution networks, process piping systems, pump calculations, HVAC systems, and hydraulic machinery. It provides a more accurate representation of fluid behavior compared to the ideal equation.

Derivation (Simple Engineering Explanation)

Bernoulli’s Equation is derived from the principle of conservation of energy applied to a flowing fluid. It states that the total mechanical energy of a fluid remains constant when energy losses are neglected.

Consider a fluid flowing through a pipe between two points. The fluid possesses three forms of mechanical energy:

  • Pressure Energy: Energy available due to the pressure exerted by the fluid.
  • Kinetic Energy: Energy associated with the movement of the fluid due to its velocity.
  • Potential Energy: Energy due to the elevation of the fluid above a reference level.

For a small mass of fluid flowing from point 1 to point 2, the total energy at point 1 must be equal to the total energy at point 2 when there is no friction or external energy interaction.

The energy balance can be written as:

Pressure Energy + Kinetic Energy + Potential Energy = Constant

The energy per unit weight of fluid is represented as:

P/ρg + V²/2g + Z = Constant

This expression is known as Bernoulli’s Equation. It shows that an increase in one form of energy results in a decrease in another form, while the total energy remains unchanged.

For example, when fluid passes through a narrow section of a pipe, the flow velocity increases due to the reduced area. The increase in velocity energy is balanced by a reduction in pressure energy, causing the pressure to decrease. This energy conversion principle is the basis of many engineering applications involving fluid flow.

In practical systems, friction and other losses reduce the available energy. Therefore, additional terms for head loss, pump input, and turbine output are included to obtain the practical Bernoulli Equation used in engineering calculations.

Applications in Engineering

Bernoulli’s Equation is widely used in engineering to analyze and design systems involving fluid movement. It helps engineers understand pressure variations, velocity changes, and energy conversion in different flow applications.

  • Pipeline Flow Analysis: Bernoulli’s Equation is used to calculate pressure changes, velocity variations, and energy losses in water, oil, gas, and process piping systems.
  • Pump System Design: Engineers use the equation along with head loss calculations to determine pump head requirements and evaluate system performance.
  • Nozzles and Orifices: The principle is applied to calculate fluid velocity and flow rates through nozzles, spray systems, and discharge openings.
  • Venturi Meters: Bernoulli’s Principle is used in Venturi meters to measure flow rate by analyzing pressure differences between the inlet and throat sections.
  • Aircraft Aerodynamics: Bernoulli’s Principle helps explain pressure differences around aircraft wings that contribute to lift generation.
  • HVAC and Ventilation Systems: The equation is used for analyzing air movement, pressure distribution, and duct system performance.
  • Hydraulic Systems: Hydraulic machines such as turbines and water distribution networks use Bernoulli’s Equation for energy analysis and system optimization.
  • Industrial Process Systems: Chemical plants, power plants, refineries, and manufacturing industries use fluid energy calculations for equipment design and operation.

Although Bernoulli’s Equation is based on simplified assumptions, it remains an important engineering tool when combined with practical considerations such as friction losses, pump efficiency, and system resistance.

Worked Example

Consider water flowing through a horizontal pipe where the pressure and velocity are known at one point. The objective is to calculate the pressure at another point using Bernoulli’s Equation.

Given:

  • Pressure at Point 1 (P₁): 200 kPa
  • Velocity at Point 1 (V₁): 2 m/s
  • Velocity at Point 2 (V₂): 5 m/s
  • Elevation Difference (Z₁ - Z₂): 0 m (Horizontal Pipe)
  • Water Density (ρ): 1000 kg/m³
  • Acceleration due to Gravity (g): 9.81 m/s²

For a horizontal pipe, the elevation terms cancel out. The Bernoulli Equation becomes:

P₁/ρg + V₁²/2g = P₂/ρg + V₂²/2g

Rearranging the equation to find pressure at Point 2:

P₂ = P₁ + ρ(V₁² - V₂²)/2

Substituting the values:

P₂ = 200000 + 1000(2² - 5²)/2

P₂ = 200000 + 1000(4 - 25)/2

P₂ = 200000 - 10500

P₂ = 189500 Pa = 189.5 kPa

Therefore, when the fluid velocity increases from 2 m/s to 5 m/s, the pressure decreases from 200 kPa to approximately 189.5 kPa. This demonstrates the energy conversion between pressure energy and velocity energy described by Bernoulli’s Principle.

Limitations

Although Bernoulli’s Equation is a powerful tool for fluid flow analysis, it has certain limitations because the ideal equation is based on simplified assumptions. In real engineering systems, fluid behavior is influenced by several factors that must be considered for accurate calculations.

  • Friction Losses: The ideal Bernoulli Equation neglects energy losses due to friction between the fluid and pipe walls. In practical systems, friction losses can significantly affect pressure and flow conditions.
  • Viscous Effects: The equation assumes an ideal fluid with negligible viscosity. Real fluids have viscosity that influences velocity distribution and energy dissipation.
  • Compressible Flow Limitations: The basic equation is mainly applicable to incompressible fluids. For high-speed gas flow or large pressure variations, compressibility effects must be considered.
  • Unsteady Flow Conditions: Bernoulli’s Equation is derived for steady flow conditions. It may not provide accurate results for rapidly changing flow systems.
  • Flow Disturbances: Turbulence, sudden expansions, contractions, valves, and fittings create additional losses that are not included in the ideal equation.
  • Energy Transfer Equipment: Pumps, turbines, compressors, and other machines add or remove energy from the fluid. These effects require the practical Bernoulli Equation with additional terms.

For accurate industrial applications, Bernoulli’s Equation should be combined with pressure drop calculations, friction factor analysis, pump performance data, and system-specific operating conditions.

Related Equations

Bernoulli’s Equation is closely related to several fundamental equations used in fluid mechanics. These equations are often applied together to analyze complete fluid flow systems and solve practical engineering problems.

  • Continuity Equation: The continuity equation is based on the conservation of mass and relates fluid velocity, flow area, and density. For incompressible flow, it is expressed as:

A₁V₁ = A₂V₂

It explains that when the flow area decreases, the fluid velocity increases to maintain the same flow rate.

  • Darcy-Weisbach Equation: This equation is used to calculate frictional pressure losses in pipes due to fluid flow resistance.

hf = f(L/D)(V²/2g)

Where hf is friction head loss, f is the Darcy friction factor, L is pipe length, and D is pipe diameter.

  • Hazen-Williams Equation: This empirical equation is commonly used for calculating pressure loss in water distribution systems and pipelines.
  • Reynolds Number Equation: Reynolds number is used to determine the flow regime and identify whether the flow is laminar, transitional, or turbulent.

Re = ρVD/μ

Where ρ is fluid density, V is velocity, D is characteristic length, and μ is dynamic viscosity.

These related equations complement Bernoulli’s Equation and are essential for practical engineering calculations involving pipelines, pumps, ducts, and industrial fluid systems.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Bernoulli’s Equation?

Bernoulli’s Equation is a fluid mechanics equation that describes the relationship between pressure, velocity, and elevation in a flowing fluid. It is based on the conservation of energy principle.

What is the main principle behind Bernoulli’s Equation?

The main principle behind Bernoulli’s Equation is the conservation of energy. It states that the total mechanical energy of an ideal flowing fluid remains constant along a streamline.

What are the three energy components in Bernoulli’s Equation?

The three energy components are pressure energy, kinetic energy, and potential energy. These are represented as pressure head, velocity head, and elevation head respectively.

When is Bernoulli’s Equation applicable?

Bernoulli’s Equation is applicable for steady flow, incompressible fluids, negligible friction losses, and flow along a streamline. For practical systems, additional loss terms are included.

Does Bernoulli’s Equation apply to gases?

Yes, Bernoulli’s Equation can be applied to gases when the density variation is negligible. For high-speed gas flows or large pressure changes, compressibility effects must be considered.

Why does pressure decrease when velocity increases?

According to Bernoulli’s Principle, when fluid velocity increases, more energy is converted into kinetic energy. If the total energy remains constant, the pressure energy decreases, resulting in lower static pressure.

What is the difference between ideal and practical Bernoulli Equation?

The ideal Bernoulli Equation assumes no energy losses, while the practical Bernoulli Equation includes friction losses, pump energy addition, and turbine energy removal to represent real engineering systems.

Can Bernoulli’s Equation be used for pipe flow calculations?

Yes, Bernoulli’s Equation is widely used for pipe flow analysis when combined with continuity equations and pressure loss calculations to determine system performance.
